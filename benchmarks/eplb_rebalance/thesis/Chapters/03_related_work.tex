\subsection{MoE communication and load balancing}

Most MoE literature treats balance at training time, routing time, or expert
placement time. Load-balancing losses and bias updates aim to prevent router
collapse \cite{fedus2021switch,wang2024lossfree}. Distributed inference adds a
systems bottleneck: expert parallelism interleaves computation with all-to-all
communication. Systems including FasterMoE, Tutel, and MegaBlocks optimize
dynamic routing, parallel layouts, or sparse expert computation
\cite{he2022fastermoe,hwang2023tutel,gale2023megablocks}. FlexMoE goes further
by changing expert placement in response to observed load
\cite{nie2023flexmoe}. These systems establish that routing skew must be handled
at runtime, but they do not schedule vLLM's background EPLB weight migration
against concurrent inference. Lina shows that skewed expert popularity creates imbalanced
transfer sizes and bandwidth demand, and reports substantial P95 inference
latency improvements from communication-aware scheduling
\cite{li2023lina}. COMET independently reports that inter-device
communication can account for up to 47\% of a MoE layer's execution time in
the evaluated models and frameworks \cite{zhang2025comet}. These measurements
motivate treating network interference as a first-class serving concern. They
do not, however, imply a universal fixed link-utilization ratio.

BASE balances token-to-expert assignments directly, while Expert Choice
gives experts fixed-size buckets and allows the number of experts selected per
token to vary \cite{lewis2021base,zhou2022expertchoice}. Both redesign routing;
neither addresses how to schedule copies after a new expert placement has
been selected. FastMoE and MoESys tackle distributed MoE execution, whereas
MoE-Infinity uses an expert cache to reduce the cost of fetching weights on
memory-constrained machines
\cite{he2021fastmoe,yu2022moesys,xue2024moeinfinity}. In this thesis, migration
instead changes which GPU ranks hold resident expert weights.

ExFlow exploits affinity between experts in successive layers to improve token
locality, whereas MoEShard splits expert computation across devices to reduce
rank imbalance \cite{yao2024exflow,balmau2025moeshard}. MegaScale-Infer separates
attention and expert computation onto different GPUs so that the two stages can
scale independently \cite{zhu2025megascaleinfer}. These approaches change where
inference computation runs or how tokens travel. Our scheduler instead takes
the placement as given and orders the weight transfers required to install it.

This work begins after EPLB has already chosen a valid new placement. It does
not change router probabilities, model outputs, or the placement objective.
Instead, it schedules the physical copying needed to realize that placement.
A placement algorithm decides \emph{what} state should move; the migration
scheduler decides \emph{when} independent transfers should share network
resources. The two decisions are complementary.

\subsection{LLM serving interference and runtime movement}

Iteration-level batching in Orca and PagedAttention in vLLM show that online
request scheduling is essential to serving efficiency
\cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023vllm}. More recent systems isolate different forms of
interference: DistServe separates prefill from decode, Sarathi-Serve uses
chunked prefills to avoid decode stalls, and Llumnix migrates live request state
between instances to improve load balance and tail latency
\cite{zhong2024distserve,agrawal2024sarathi,sun2024llumnix}. The present work
addresses a different object and time scale. It does not move requests or KV
caches and does not change request batching; it schedules expert-weight copies
that arise only when EPLB installs a new placement.

Splitwise and Mooncake also separate prompt processing from generation;
Mooncake makes KV-cache placement and transfer central to serving
\cite{patel2023splitwise,qin2024mooncake}. SGLang reuses shared prefixes,
while FlexGen trades latency for throughput by scheduling computation and
offload across a GPU, host memory, and disk
\cite{zheng2023sglang,sheng2023flexgen}. Clockwork focuses on predictable
latency, and AlpaServe uses model parallelism to absorb bursts across models
\cite{gujarati2020clockwork,li2023alpaserve}. Together these studies illustrate
why the benefit of an execution policy should be judged under its target
workload and latency objective, not from isolated operation times alone.

\subsection{Collective-flow scheduling}

A set of expert migrations resembles a coflow: several point-to-point flows
share a common completion or interference objective
\cite{chowdhury2012coflow}. Varys models coflow
completion through the most heavily loaded ingress or egress endpoint and the
remaining measured bandwidth at that endpoint \cite{chowdhury2014varys}. This
bottleneck view is closely related to the maximum normalized link-load term in
the ILP developed in \cref{ch:formulation}. The present model is intentionally
simpler: it uses fixed routes and a normalized foreground-traffic parameter,
and it optimizes a serving-interference surrogate rather than coflow
completion time. Aalo instead aims to reduce coflow completion times
without advance knowledge of flow sizes \cite{chowdhury2015aalo}; Philae
estimates coflow sizes by sampling a subset of their flows
\cite{jajoo2019philae}. Here the
migration flows are known when the plan is built; the uncertain quantity is
the bandwidth available while inference is in progress.

\subsection{Graph scheduling and expert migration}

A migration layer can be represented by a graph whose vertices are ranks and
whose directed edges are remote flows. If each rank may participate in at most
one flow in a batch, edges sharing an endpoint cannot receive the same batch
identifier. This is an edge-coloring constraint \cite{west2001graph}. Transfer
direction and expert identity remain necessary for execution, but direction
does not change whether two flows contend at an endpoint. Each generated batch
is a matching in this rank graph. Finding the minimum number of colors
in a general graph is computationally hard \cite{holyer1981edge}; this
result applies to minimum edge coloring, not directly to the network-based
ILP in \cref{ch:formulation}.

Exact edge coloring alone is not the complete systems objective: topology,
transfer sizes, replica choices, and foreground traffic also matter. The
deployed first-fit rule is therefore a conservative, topology-independent
heuristic rather than a claim of general optimality. It is inspired by the
migration grouping used in OmniInfer's placement manager
\cite{omniinferplacement}. The contribution of this thesis is the explicit
interference formulation and the integration of this rule into vLLM's
asynchronous EPLB path, including deterministic flow construction, NIXL
lifecycle handling, cycle-wide schedule precomputation, correctness tests,
and end-to-end serving evaluation.

\subsection{Positioning of this thesis}

Prior MoE systems primarily optimize token dispatch, expert computation, or
placement, while coflow systems optimize transfer completion and LLM-serving
systems schedule requests or KV-cache movement. This thesis begins after an
EPLB policy has selected a placement and asks how to realize that decision
without producing avoidable NIC bursts during inference. Its distinct
contribution is the combination of an interference-oriented formulation, a
dependency-free rank-pair batching rule, communicator-aware execution, and an
upstream vLLM implementation evaluated with both serving and NIC measurements.
